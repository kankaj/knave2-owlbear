# Running the game (pp. 1–3, 68–74)

## What Knave is (p. 1)

An old-school fantasy RPG in the Braunstein / Blackmoor / D&D tradition, broadly compatible with fifty years of monsters, items, and adventures. Play is open-ended: PCs leave a safe haven, search dangerous places for treasure, and return to carouse and recover. Anything may be attempted; survival is not guaranteed. The rules are a starting point and are expected to be edited by the table.

**Tables.** Nearly every element of fantasy is broken into 100 examples. Roll d100 or pick. Italic entries refer to another table — roll there and interpret in light of the original; if that also gives a table reference, take the next non-table entry. Combining 2–3 tables gives the best results ("donkey" + "porcupine" = a quilled horse-thing); more than that gets nonsensical.

## Game Master duties (p. 2)

- **Create locations to explore** — seeded with treasures, traps, friends, foes, monsters, devices, secrets, problems without obvious solutions, and powder-keg situations. Non-linear, multiple routes.
- **Flesh out the supporting cast** — personalities, goals, fears, loyalties, motivations, entangled together.
- **Let the players guide the action** — no plot; each session's outcome surprises everyone.
- **Keep the game moving** — point at players and ask; appoint a "caller" if debate drags.
- **Use common sense** — most actions simply succeed or fail; avoid rolling for everything.
- **Make impartial, consistent rulings** — players play against the world, not the GM; roll in the open unless the players wouldn't know the result.
- **Immerse the players** — a living, reactive, internally consistent world; use the generators.
- **Reveal the world** — give plenty of information; when in doubt, more. Choices need information.
- **Signpost danger** — the more dangerous, the more obvious. Never punish the unavoidable.
- **Reward smart plans** — clever plans that remove an obstacle can simply succeed.
- **Keep time records** — time is a resource; track supplies, rival factions, advancing threats.
- **Edit the rules** — the rules serve the table.

## Player duties (p. 3)

- **Assist the GM** — be on time, learn the rules, keep notes, draw maps, state plans at session end so the GM can prep.
- **Create a character** that fits the campaign; distinctive features, no elaborate backstory — the game is what happens in play.
- **Play your character** — first or third person, voice optional; cooperate; no intra-party conflict without consent.
- **Take initiative** — set goals, seek adventure.
- **Ask questions** — information is the lifeblood; search, visit libraries, interrogate, consult sages.
- **Apply tactical infinity** — treat the world as real; no detail is merely flavor.
- **Scheme** — think laterally; build plans so bulletproof no roll is needed; use psychology, magic, allies, gear, terrain.
- **Fight dirty** — win before the fight starts; avoid fights without the upper hand; combat is neither balanced nor fair.
- **Prepare to die** — embrace it, roll a new PC; the campaign tells the story of a world, not one character.

## Gameplay example (p. 68), condensed

Two PCs, Arthur (spear, torch) and Bridget (sword), enter a tomb. A stone chamber: four marble busts in wall niches, a fifth in the centre (Cornelius, Duke of Harfax), an iron portcullis north. Lifting the portcullis fails outright — no roll, it is locked. Bridget **searches** (one turn, torchlight); Arthur examines the busts and takes the central one (2 slots — marble is heavy). The turn ends → **hazard die 4**, a delve shift: cold wind through the portcullis. The search reveals seams on the four wall busts' necks; heads rotate; turning all four north at once opens the portcullis.

Moving at **crawling speed**, they reach a T-junction, go east, and a turn ends → **hazard die 1**, encounter: two skeletons 40′ away. **Surprise**: Arthur (WIS 3) checks vs the skeletons' WIS 0 (target 11), rolls 17 — a margin of 5+, so the party has surprised them: they act first with +5 on all combat checks this round. Bridget charges: d20 15 + 5 surprise + 2 STR = 22 vs the skeleton's AC 12 — a hit, and **21+** grants a free maneuver. She declares a **power attack** (2d6, sword breaks): 3 damage; the maneuver knocks the skeleton prone. Arthur nearly stabs a skeleton with a spear (piercing — skeletons are immune), thinks again, and drops the marble bust on it: the GM rules no roll is needed. Then the surviving skeleton acts.

Lessons the example teaches: searching costs time not rolls; the hazard die drives the pace; surprise is decided by a single opposed WIS check; 21+ = free maneuver; power attacks trade the weapon for dice; immunities matter; a clever use of the environment bypasses the dice.

## Designer's commentary (p. 71), the parts that affect rulings

- **Goals.** Knave began as a Basic D&D hack for 5th-graders: learn the rules and make a PC in minutes. Inspirations: World of Dungeons, Into the Odd, The Black Hack.
- **Ability scores** run 0–10 rather than 3–18 and are added to a d20 against a usual target of 16: low-level success chance is 25–40 %, rising 5 % per point, matching classic saving-throw and attack progression. Adding 11 turns any score into a target number, which is what makes every check **reversible** — a group can have only the players roll.
- **Classlessness.** The archetype linked to each ability lets players design their own multi-class by where they put points; every ability is meant to matter so there is no dump stat.
- **Careers** give background and a loadout without shopping. Starting PCs may take expensive armor, but its resale value is tiny because armor is tailored.
- **Wounds and item slots** split damage into a short-term pool (HP) and a long-term pool (wounds) and leave a record of injuries; the GM may make some wounds heal slower or need treatment. Starting PCs are tougher than in most fantasy RPGs; d6 HP per level evens it out later.
- **Hazard system** adapted from Brendan S. (Necropraxis), CC BY 3.0.
- **Six-mile hexes**: roughly what one sees from a flat plain; searchable in a watch; three hexes ≈ an 18-mile hiking day.
- **Delving**: 120′/turn is so slow that trap detection is automatic at that speed; searching costs time, not rolls; candles trade search speed for burn time and weight; a lantern protects a candle at the cost of a slot.
- **Encounters**: the activities table makes the world feel alive; the reaction table is deliberately specific to help roleplay.
- **Combat** depletes gear — HP, ammo, weapons (natural 1s, power attacks). Optional "hard mode": an item breaks when its slot is wounded. Fighters should carry several weapons for breakage and for exploiting weaknesses. High rolls give maneuvers to encourage creativity.
- **Spellcasting** is level-less so every found book is usable; the 100 spells are non-damaging utility to encourage devious play. Damage spells generated by the tables: about INT × d6.
- **Relic magic** is deliberately freeform and relational; it hands the GM mission hooks and moral quandaries, and serving several patrons may prove impossible.
- **Alchemy**: "let the players make it up" instead of hundreds of formulae.
- **Money**: coarse price categories; no denominations; 1 gp elsewhere = 1c here, so wealth comes from unique treasures and connections rather than coin piles.
- **Buildings** double as a dungeon-building kit and as mission rewards; every party needs a base.
- **Warfare** rules also resolve warband skirmishes.
- **Downtime**: carousing-for-XP after Jeff Rients; gambling after En Garde!.
- **Recruiting**: hiring many followers is fine — hirelings flee, mercenaries refuse dungeons, and open warbands provoke factions.
- **Monsters**: keep them simple; think about the signs, sounds, and smells that foreshadow them.
- **Saving throws** from other games become ability checks, usually vs 16: STR (hold/paralysis), DEX (breath, blasts, rays, wands), CON (poison, death, petrification, polymorph), INT (magical devices), WIS (spells, illusions).
